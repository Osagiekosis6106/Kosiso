### Part 1

December 1945. A bomber is falling out of the sky over Maryland.

Both engines have quit. The crew have to jump. But this bomber has a problem no other bomber has.

Its propellers aren't on the wings. They're on the tail. Right behind the crew. Spinning in the exact spot anyone bailing out would fall through.

So before the last man can leave, the pilot has to do something no bomber pilot had ever done. He has to get rid of the propellers.

This is the Douglas XB-42 Mixmaster.

So why would anyone put a bomber's propellers on its tail? Not on the wings. Not on the nose. Behind everything.

The answer is one word. And by the end of this video, you'll see why it made the Mixmaster one of the fastest piston bombers ever built… and why it still lost.

It starts in 1943.

The Second World War is being fought by bombers. And bombers have one enemy that never takes a day off. Fighters. A slow bomber needs gunners, armour and escorts just to survive.

So engineers at Douglas, in California, asked a different question. What if a bomber was simply too fast to catch?

Britain had already shown it could work. Its wooden Mosquito was outrunning German fighters. But to be fast, a bomber needs a lot of power. And power means engines. And engines, on a normal bomber, hang on the wings.

That's the problem. And it has a name.

Drag.

Every engine on a wing sits in its own pod, called a nacelle. Every nacelle is a big, blunt shape pushed through the air. And every propeller in front of a wing throws a twisting, churning blast of air back over it.

So a twin-engine bomber isn't one clean shape. It's three. A fuselage and two engine pods, all fighting the wind.

Douglas didn't wait to be asked. In May 1943, it took its own idea to the Army Air Forces. And the Army ordered two prototypes.

The obvious answer to our question is: Douglas put the propellers on the tail to go faster.

That's only half right.

### Part 2

Here's what Douglas actually built.

Behind the cockpit, inside the fuselage, sit two Allison V-12 engines. The same family of engine that powered the P-38 and the P-40. Not on the wings. Inside the body.

From each engine, a long drive shaft runs back through the fuselage to a gearbox in the tail. And there, two propellers, three blades each, spin in opposite directions. Each engine drives one of them.

That's where the nickname comes from. From the side, it looks like a kitchen mixer. The Mixmaster.

Now look at the wing.

Nothing on it. No engine pods. No propellers in front of it. The air meets a smooth, clean wing and slides over it undisturbed. Even the air for the engines comes in through thin slots in the wing's leading edge.

From the front, this two-engine bomber has roughly the frontal shape of a single-engine fighter.

And there's a second gift. Both propellers sit on the centreline. If one engine fails on a normal twin, the good engine pulls the plane sideways. On the Mixmaster, the push stays in the middle. It was designed to keep flying on one engine.

On the 6th of May 1944, the first XB-42 flew from Palm Springs, California.

And it was fast. About 410 miles an hour. 660 kilometres an hour. With room inside for up to 8,000 pounds of bombs. That's more than three and a half tonnes.

That's close to what the best fighters of 1944 could do. A medium bomber, running at fighter speed.

But put propellers at the back of an aircraft, and you collect a set of problems nobody else has.

Problem one. The air.

A propeller on the nose bites into clean, smooth air. A propeller on the tail has to work in everything the aircraft has already stirred up. Open the bomb doors, or lower the landing gear and flaps, and that air gets rough. The propellers started to shake. Badly enough that some test crews thought it was dangerous.

Problem two. The ground.

When a normal plane takes off, it lifts its nose and rotates. The Mixmaster can't rotate much. Tip the nose up, and the tail drops, and the propellers hit the runway. So Douglas added a fin under the tail, with a bumper on it, to keep the blades off the ground. Takeoffs had to be long and flat.

Problem three. Getting out.

If you have to jump from a normal bomber, the propellers are out on the wings, away from you. On the Mixmaster, they're right where you'd fall. So Douglas built in a last resort. Before bailing out, the crew could jettison the propellers and the tail gearbox, and throw them away.

Keep that in mind.

### Part 3

The war in Europe ended. Then the war in the Pacific. And the Mixmaster had never gone into production.

But Douglas still had something to prove.

On the 8th of December 1945, the second XB-42 took off from Long Beach, California, with Lieutenant Colonel Henry Warden and Captain Glen Edwards at the controls.

It landed at Bolling Field, in Washington, D.C., about 2,300 miles away. 3,700 kilometres.

Five hours, seventeen minutes.

That's an average of 433 miles an hour. Nearly 700 kilometres an hour. Helped along by strong winds at altitude, but a coast-to-coast speed record.

Glen Edwards would die less than three years later, testing another experimental bomber: Northrop's flying wing. Today, the base where America tests its new aircraft carries his name. Edwards Air Force Base.

Eight days after the record, the same aircraft took off from Bolling Field again. Lieutenant Colonel Fred Ascani was flying, with two other officers aboard.

Within minutes, everything went wrong.

The landing gear wouldn't come down properly. The left engine failed. Then, as the coolant got hotter and hotter, the right engine failed too.

A bomber with no engines… and propellers on its tail.

The other two jumped first. Then Ascani did the thing this aircraft had been designed for. He went back, jettisoned the propellers… and jumped at about four hundred feet.

All three men survived. The record-setting Mixmaster was destroyed in a field at Oxon Hill, Maryland.

Fred Ascani would go on to set a world speed record of his own, in a jet.

And that's the clue to how the Mixmaster story ends.

Which brings us back to the question. Why did the XB-42 have its propellers on its tail?

Here's the physics.

Speed is a fight between thrust and drag. Push harder, or slip through the air more easily.

Douglas couldn't get much more push from piston engines. So it attacked the other side. It took the two engine pods off the wings, buried the engines in the body, and put both propellers behind everything, where they couldn't spoil the airflow over the wing.

Two engines' worth of power. Close to one fighter's worth of drag.

That's the full answer. The propellers went on the tail so that nothing would be in the way of the wing.

And it worked. But only a propeller could ever need that trick.

Because while Douglas was perfecting the propeller… the world was giving up on it.

The Army had already asked Douglas to add jets to the Mixmaster. In 1947, the surviving prototype flew again as the XB-42A, with two small jet engines under its wings. They added so much drag that its range fell by about 650 miles.

Meanwhile, Douglas had taken the same fuselage, removed the piston engines and propellers, and put jet engines inside. It was called the XB-43 Jetmaster. One of America's first jet bombers.

No propellers to hide. No shafts. No gearbox. No exploding tail.

The Mixmaster had solved the propeller problem perfectly… just as aircraft stopped needing propellers.

The Air Force cancelled it in 1948.

The surviving fuselage reached the National Museum of the US Air Force in 2010. Its wings, removed to move it, were later lost. And in October 2026, after decades in storage, it went on public display for the first time.

But the idea never really died. Look at a modern MQ-9 Reaper drone. Engine in the body. One propeller at the back.

And ten years after the Mixmaster, the Navy took two propellers spinning in opposite directions, put them on the nose of a fighter, stood it on its tail… and asked its pilot to land it backwards.

That story is right here.
