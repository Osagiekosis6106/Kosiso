Research date: 24 Sep 2026. Everything below was pulled with NexLev (channel stats, transcripts, comments, similar-channel search, outlier search, geography and revenue estimates) and YouTube search. Every number in this file comes from those pulls. Where a fact still needs checking before you publish it, it's marked **(verify)**.

## 1. The short version

1. **Airspeed is not an AI channel.** Its About page, every description and its pinned comment say *"Every video is created entirely by humans."* The credits name seven people: three 3D animators, an editor and sound designer, a researcher and writer, a narrator and two directors. It buys royalty-free 3D aircraft models and animates them. You are up against a small studio, not a prompt.
2. **The copycats lost on packaging, timing and trust, not on script.** Six channels re-made the Spirit of St. Louis video. Between them they have about 5,200 views, against Airspeed's 1.28M. Two of the copycat scripts are *more* dramatic than Airspeed's. They lost because they copied the one topic YouTube had already served to everyone who wanted it, and wrapped it in loud AI thumbnails on channels with no audience.
3. **Airspeed isn't original either.** Vintage Wings put out *"The Boeing 377 Stratocruiser Was Luxurious, But Dangerous"* on 2 Aug 2026 and got 516K views (a 24.9× outlier). Airspeed put out *"Why the Boeing 377 Failed"* three weeks later. Airspeed's real method is **take a proven topic → apply premium 3D and a clean "Why did X have Y?" package → chain the videos together.**
4. **To beat them, copy the method, not the video.** Pick proven aircraft with one strange part you can *see*. Package them better than Airspeed. Use visuals that *show* the answer, which Airspeed only *tells*. Be more accurate than Airspeed (its top comments are corrections). Publish more often and use Shorts, which Airspeed doesn't.

## 3. Decoding Airspeed's formula

### 3a. Title formula

`Why [Did] the [Aircraft] [Have / Look Like / Fail] [one strange, visible trait]?`

- Name the aircraft. Aviation fans search by name, and the name is also the search keyword.
- The trait has to be **visible and slightly absurd**: no front window, two fighters joined together, enormous.
- It's a question, so a viewer who can't answer it feels a small itch.
- It stays under ~55 characters and uses no caps-lock, emoji or "SHOCKING". The audience is 25–54-year-old men who dislike clickbait.

### 3b. Thumbnail formula (compare your screenshots)

| Airspeed | Copycats |
|---|---|
| One aircraft, dead-centre, often **symmetrical head-on** | Aircraft at a random angle over an ocean |
| **Dark studio gradient**, navy fading to crimson, with a rim light | Busy sky or sea background |
| **No text. No arrows.** | "NO WINDOW?!", red arrows, yellow meme font |
| Looks like a museum piece or a product render | Looks like every other AI channel |
| The same look on every video, so it reads as a series | No consistent look |

The weird trait is **shown, not labelled.** Head-on, the Spirit of St. Louis plainly has no windscreen, and the viewer's brain does the rest. That's why it outperforms text.

### 3c. Script formula (from the Spirit, F-82 and 377 transcripts)

About 1,600–1,800 words, spoken at ~150 words per minute, runs 10–12 minutes. Here's the Spirit video's structure:

| Time | Beat | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | **Question hook** | Restate the title with a three-part negation: *"Not a narrow slit, not a tiny windshield, but nothing directly ahead of the pilot at all."* Then the contradiction: *"And then he used that aircraft to fly alone across the Atlantic."* |
| 0:30–1:05 | **Stakes and promise** | *"…the answer to why Lindbergh could not see forward begins with the problem everyone else was trying to solve."* |
| 1:05–2:40 | **The problem of the era** | Why ordinary aircraft couldn't do the job: the Orteig Prize, and rivals who chose bigger planes. |
| 2:40–4:10 | **Spec sheet** | Length, height, wingspan, ceiling, cruise speed, range, crew, while the 3D model turns. Metric first. |
| 4:10–6:45 | **Front-to-back walkaround** | *"At the front was… Directly behind the engine…"* A mid-point open loop: *"But that alone doesn't explain…"* |
| 6:45–9:00 | **The mission** | Takeoff, fog, ice, dead reckoning, Ireland, Paris. |
| 9:00–10:05 | **Afterlife** | Tours, the Smithsonian, "a one-mission machine". |
| 10:05–11:20 | **The payoff** | *"Which brings us back to the question…"* ending on one quotable line: *"While a forward window made flying easier, fuel made Paris possible."* |
| 11:20–end | **Chain tease** | *"But two decades later… Find out why by watching our video on the Bell X-1 now."* |

Every video ends by pointing to another (F-82 → Spirit → X-1; 377 → X-1). That creates a **binge loop**, and it's a big part of why a new channel has a 200K floor.

There are **no** subscribe asks, sponsor reads or "smash like" moments. The only direct ask is a pinned comment question: *"If you were Lindbergh, what would worry you most…?"* It has 120 likes and 44 replies.

### 3d. Visual and sound formula (from a NexLev analysis of the first 2.5 minutes)

- Average shot length is **~4.5 seconds**, with mostly hard cuts and slow orbits.
- **3D hero shots:** a realistic hangar "studio" with *AIRSPEED* painted on the wall (branding inside the render), plus realistic sky and ocean scenes with time-of-day changes to show a long flight.
- **A faceless blue mannequin pilot** in the cockpit. It gives scale and a point of view without anyone's face.
- **Archival film** cut between the 3D shots: grainy 1920s black-and-white from the Internet Archive, the National Archives and Wikimedia Commons, all credited in the description.
- **Minimal beige maps** with animated routes and large distance labels in a clean sans-serif.
- **Macro close-ups** of rivets, tail markings and engine cylinders.
- **Narration:** a calm American male voice at moderate pace, the "museum docent" tone.
- **Sound:** a constant propeller drone and wind whooshes, with low orchestral music that builds tension.
- **Description template:** a summary paragraph, chapters, crew credits, "3D assets purchased under royalty-free licences", and a list of sources with licences.

### Why they failed

1. **The demand was already used up.** By the time they uploaded (8–20 days later), YouTube had shown the Spirit video to about 1.28M people. Anyone curious about the missing window had already had their answer. A copy is competing for leftovers.
2. **Their search results sit under the original.** Search *"why did the spirit of st louis have no front window"* and Airspeed is #1 with 1.2M views. A 0-subscriber channel with the same title is never picked over that.
3. **The thumbnails signal "cheap AI channel".** Red arrows, "NO WINDOW?!" text and yellow meme fonts fall straight into the style this 25–54 male audience has learned to scroll past. Airspeed's calm studio render signals quality.
4. **No channel momentum.** A brand-new channel's first video is shown to a tiny test audience. Airspeed launched three strong videos at once and got a 200K floor. The copycats launched one weak, derivative video.
5. **Wrong facts in the packaging.** Artillery Club's title says "Any Windows" (it had side windows) and "The First Plane to Cross the Atlantic" (Alcock and Brown crossed nonstop in 1919). Aviation fans punish that in comments and by not clicking.
6. **Wrong length.** 21-, 41- and 14-minute versions of a question Airspeed answered in 11:39. Padding kills retention.
7. **It isn't the scripts.** MachForge opened with *"When aeronautical engineer Donald Hall drew up the blueprints… he handed Charles Lindbergh a death sentence."* and explained the centre-of-gravity physics better than Airspeed did, and still got 10 views. **A great script in a copied topic with a weak package still dies.**

**Rule:** never re-make someone's outlier inside the same 90 days. Make **the next video in the same pattern** about a different aircraft.

## 5. Airspeed's weak spots (where you win)

These come from their top comments and a close read of the transcripts.

1. **Accuracy slips, in a niche full of experts.**
   - Top-liked correction (568 likes, 61 replies): *"Alcock and Brown already flew non-stop across the Atlantic in 1919, Lindbergh was the first to do it solo."* Airspeed's "race across the Atlantic" section never mentions them.
   - A detailed comment pulls apart their reasoning: tank placement near the centre of gravity was about **trim as fuel burned**, not crash safety, and "a high wing gives more lift" is wrong.
   - *"He hadn't slept for more like 2.5 days, not 1.5."*

   Corrections drive engagement, but they cap trust. **Your edge is to be the channel that gets it right**, with a researcher's checklist (section 9).
2. **The spec-sheet dump (2:40–4:10) is dead air.** It's a list of numbers over a turning model, which is the classic place viewers drop off. Turn specs into comparisons: *"a wingspan wider than a city bus is long"*.
3. **They tell the answer instead of showing it.** The real reason for the missing window (the fuel tank at the centre of gravity) gets two sentences and no visual. An X-ray animation of the tank draining while the CG marker stays still would *be* the payoff.
4. **The answer comes too late.** The payoff arrives at 85–90%, and the middle drifts into general history. Give a partial answer at ~25% and the full one at ~85%.
5. **No human voice.** Lindbergh wrote two books about this flight (*We*, 1927; *The Spirit of St. Louis*, 1953, which won a Pulitzer), and Airspeed quotes neither. Real quotes read over 3D give an emotional lift the copycats can't fake.
6. **Metric first for a 53%-American audience.** Give both units (*"about 14 metres, 46 feet"*) or lead with imperial for US-built aircraft.
7. **One flat tone.** It's always the calm docent voice. Add two or three tension beats per video: silence, engine cut-out, a heartbeat.
8. **No Shorts, and slow cadence.** Airspeed gets 0 Shorts views. Each long video could feed three vertical 30–45 s clips of its best 3D moment.
9. **Low RPM (~$3.3).** India is 18% of viewers, and history and animation topics earn less. Lean slightly toward **US military and Cold War aircraft**, which draw more US and UK viewers and more defence-adjacent advertisers.

- Same audience and same "Why did X have Y?" hook.

### Honesty about AI (this is important)

- Airspeed's "made entirely by humans" line is a **selling point to this audience**. If you use AI, don't hide it and **never claim "no AI"**.

- **YouTube's "inauthentic content" monetisation rule** (the old "repetitious content" rule) removes channels that look mass-produced. What protects you is an original, researched script, a consistent narrator and real editing. The copycats are what that rule targets.

## 8. Production pipeline: AI plus real 3D, done right

Current AI video models can't keep aircraft geometry exact. Propellers morph, engine counts change and markings melt. **The aviation audience will spot every error in the comments.** So split the work:

| Shot type | Use | Tools |
|---|---|---|
| **Hero aircraft** (turntables, walkarounds, cutaways, the X-ray moment, thumbnail) | **A real 3D model** | Buy a model (CGTrader, TurboSquid, Sketchfab; check the licence allows commercial video, as Airspeed does). Render in **Blender** (free) with a studio HDRI from **Poly Haven** (free, CC0). |
| **Moving hero shots** (flying through cloud, hook-ups, takeoff) | Your own Blender render, **or** image-to-video from **your own render** as the first frame | Kling, Veo or Runway image-to-video. Always start from your accurate render, never from text alone. |
| **Atmosphere** (storm clouds, night ocean, 1940s airfield, crowds, cockpit gauges) | AI image and AI video | Midjourney, Flux or GPT-Image for stills, then Kling or Veo for 3–5 s motion |
| **Real history** (the real people, test film, newspapers) | **Archival** | National Archives (catalog.archives.gov), Internet Archive, Wikimedia Commons, **SDASM Archives on Flickr** (San Diego Air & Space Museum, huge, "no known copyright restrictions"), the US Air Force's National Museum site, Library of Congress |
| **Maps, spec cards, comparisons** | Motion graphics | After Effects, CapCut, or Blender text |
| **Narration** | Human voice (best), or one consistent AI voice | Record yourself or hire on Fiverr / Voices.com. If AI, use ElevenLabs with **one** fixed voice. |
| **Sound** | An engine bed for each aircraft type, whooshes, low orchestral score | Epidemic Sound or Artlist (licensed), YouTube Audio Library |

For Library of Congress and Prelinger material you can use the repo's `archival_fetch.py` (guide: `archival-fetch-guide.md`). For example, `python3 archival_fetch.py "B-29" --source loc --count 15`. These commands couldn't be test-run from this session, so check what comes back.

**Budget path.** Month 1: Blender plus 3–4 bought models (~$20–80 each), plus one AI video subscription, plus a stock music subscription. That makes one video a week possible for one person once you've practised the Blender basics: a turntable, a camera path, a transparent-material X-ray.

### 8a. Is a 3D model a video? No. Here's the difference

| Word | What it is | File |
|---|---|---|
| **3D model** | The aircraft itself as a digital object. You can turn it, light it and film it from any angle, like a real toy in a room. | `.blend`, `.fbx`, `.obj`, `.glb` |
| **Render (still)** | A photo you take of the model inside Blender | `.png` |
| **Render (animation)** | A video you film of the model inside Blender, with a moving camera or moving parts | `.mp4`, or a folder of numbered `.png` frames |

So every 3D shot goes one of two ways:

- **`[3D]` Blender-only:** you animate the camera or the parts in Blender and render the **video** directly. Use this for turntables, walkarounds, wings folding, propellers spinning, the X-ray, and anything where the aircraft's shape must stay perfect. **No AI prompt needed.**
- **`[3D→AI]` Blender still, then AI motion:** you render **one still** in Blender, upload it to Kling, Veo or Runway as the **first frame** ("start frame" or "image-to-video"), and write a motion prompt. Use this when the motion is hard in Blender: clouds, dust, turbulence, shattering glass, fire. Keep these clips to **3–5 seconds**, because the longer they run, the more the aircraft morphs.

### 8b. Blender in 15 minutes (the only skills you need)

**Download (free):** https://www.blender.org/download/ · **Manual:** https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/latest/ · **Free studio lighting (HDRIs):** https://polyhaven.com/hdris · **Models:** https://sketchfab.com and https://www.cgtrader.com (filter by *downloadable* and a licence that allows commercial use).

For a guided start, search YouTube for **"Blender Guru donut tutorial"**, the standard beginner course. Parts 1–3 are enough.

1. **Set up.** Install and open Blender, then choose **General**. Click the default cube and press **X → Delete**.
2. **Bring in the aircraft.** Go to **File → Import** and pick the format you bought (`.fbx`, `.obj` or `.glb`/glTF). Press **N** to open the side panel and check **Dimensions**. Scale the model so its length matches the real aircraft (XF-85: ~4.52 m, verify).
3. **Look around.** Middle mouse button orbits, scroll zooms, and Shift + middle mouse pans. Press **Numpad 1 / 3 / 7** for front, side and top views.
4. **Dark studio (Airspeed look, in your colours):**
   - **Add → Mesh → Plane**. Scale it big and bend it up behind the aircraft (a "cyclorama"), or skip it and just set the world colour to near-black under **World properties → Color**.
   - Add **three lights** with **Add → Light → Area**: one soft white key light in front and above, plus two **rim lights** behind in your two brand colours (for example amber and steel blue).
   - For realistic outdoor shots, go to **World properties → Color → Environment Texture** and open a Poly Haven sky HDRI instead.
5. **Camera.**
   - **Add → Camera**, then press **Numpad 0** to look through it.
   - Press **N → View → tick "Camera to View"**, then move around to frame the shot. Untick it when you're done.
   - Under **Output properties**, set 1920×1080 (or 3840×2160) and **Frame Rate 24 or 30**.
6. **Animate the camera (keyframes).**
   - At frame 1, select the camera and press **I → Location & Rotation**.
   - Go to frame 120 (4 s at 30 fps), move the camera, and press **I** again. Blender fills in the motion between them.
7. **Turntable (the classic orbit).**
   - Add an **Empty** (**Add → Empty → Plain Axes**) at the aircraft's centre.
   - Select the camera, Shift-click the Empty, then press **Ctrl + P → Object**.
   - Key the Empty's **Rotation Z = 0°** at frame 1 and **360°** at frame 240.
   - In the **Graph Editor**, select all and press **T → Linear** so the speed stays constant.
8. **Moving parts** (wings folding, hook rising, propellers):
   - This only works if the bought model has them as **separate objects**. Check the **Outliner** (top right).
   - Set the part's pivot at its hinge: right-click → **Set Origin → Origin to 3D Cursor** after putting the 3D cursor on the hinge with **Shift + right-click**.
   - Then key its rotation, as in step 6.
9. **X-ray (your trademark).**
   - Select the fuselage, open **Material properties**, and set **Alpha** to ~0.15 (in Eevee, also set the material's *Render Method* to *Blended* if it looks solid).
   - Give the part you want to reveal (engine, fuel tank, centre-of-gravity marker) an **Emission** colour so it glows.
   - For a blueprint look, use **Add Modifier → Generate → Wireframe**.
10. **Render.**
    - Set the render engine to **Eevee**, which is fast and good enough for most shots. Use **Cycles** only for hero shots.
    - **Still:** **F12**, then **Image → Save As → PNG**. Use this for thumbnails and `[3D→AI]` first frames.
    - **Video:** go to **Output properties → Output**, choose a folder and set **File Format: PNG** (safest). Then run **Render → Render Animation** (Ctrl + F12). Drop the numbered PNGs into CapCut or DaVinci Resolve as an image sequence.
11. **Save your studio** as `studio.blend`. Next video, open it, import the new aircraft, and you're 80% done.

**If a model you need doesn't exist to buy:** hire a modeller on Fiverr or CGTrader custom requests (~$50–250 for a mid-detail aircraft). Send them 3-view drawings and photos, and ask for **separate objects** for the wings, hook, canopy, propellers and control surfaces.

### 8c. The prompt-labelling rule (use on every script)

Every visual line in every script must carry **three labels**:

1. **Type.** One of these:
   - `[3D]` Blender-only animation. No AI prompt.
   - `[3D→AI]` Blender still, then an image-to-video prompt.
   - `[AI]` AI direct: text-to-image, then image-to-video.
   - `[ARCH]` archival.
   - `[GFX]` graphic.
   - `[X-RAY]` Blender, your trademark shot.
2. **⚠ VERIFY or ✓ No check.** Mark a shot **⚠ VERIFY** when viewers would notice if the image were wrong: the aircraft's shape, markings, number of engines or fins, the real location, the real event, a real person. Say **what** to check and **against which reference photo or source**. Mark mood-only shots **✓ No check**.
3. **Disclosure.** If a realistic AI shot shows a **real event or real person**, mark **Disclosure ON**. That means switching on YouTube's "altered or synthetic content" at upload.

**The golden rule:** *if the named aircraft (or any real, identifiable aircraft or person) is recognisable in the frame, it is never `[AI]` direct.* It is `[3D]` or `[3D→AI]`, or `[ARCH]`. AI direct is only for skies, landscapes, weather, crowds from behind, silhouettes and generic modern scenes.

**Image-to-video prompt structure** (Kling, Veo, Runway, with your render uploaded as the first frame):
`[camera move] + [what moves] + [what must NOT change] + [look] + duration 3–5 s`
Always end with: *"Keep the aircraft's shape, markings, number of engines and propellers exactly as in the first frame. No morphing. Realistic motion blur."*

### 8d. No Blender? Use Grok

`airspeed-grok-prompts.md` gives every 3D shot of video #1 as a Grok Imagine image and video prompt. It also says, shot by shot, whether to upload an existing master image (**REUSE**), generate a new image from one (**NEW**) or continue from the last frame of the previous clip (**CHAIN**), so the aircraft looks the same all video. For future scripts, build the same set: download real photos, make approved masters (M1, M2…), write lock text for each aircraft, then write one shot table.

### Shot rhythm to copy and improve

- 3.5–4.5 s average shot, with hard cuts on narration beats.
- About 60% 3D, 20% archival, 10% graphics and maps, 10% AI atmosphere.
- **One "X-ray" moment** of 20–40 s at the payoff.
- Put **scale objects** in the 3D scene (a man, a bus, a B-29 next to the fighter). Size contrast holds attention.

## 9. Your improved script formula ("Airspeed+")

Target 1,650–1,900 words at 150 wpm, for **11–13 minutes**. That's long enough for mid-rolls (8 min+) and short enough to keep retention.

| % of video | Beat | Improvement over Airspeed |
|---|---|---|
