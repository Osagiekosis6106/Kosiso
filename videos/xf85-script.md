# Why Did This Fighter Have No Landing Gear?

**Status: 🔒 LOCKED** (25 Sep 2026). Final and fact-checked (see `xf85-fact-check.md`). Record this version word for word. Any change needs a new fact-check. ~1,200 words, about 8–9 minutes of narration at 140–150 words per minute.

---

Why would anyone build a fighter jet with no landing gear?

Not small wheels. Not wheels that fold away. No wheels at all.

Just a hook… on the top of its nose.

Because this fighter was never supposed to take off from a runway, or land on one. Its airfield was going to be another aircraft, flying at over 200 miles an hour.

And the first time a pilot tried to fly back to it… the airfield nearly killed him.

This is the McDonnell XF-85 Goblin, the smallest American jet fighter ever built. It was about the length of a family car, and shaped like an egg with wings. It only makes sense once you understand the problem it was built to solve, a problem big enough that the Air Force seriously considered hiding fighters inside its bombers.

At the end of World War Two, the United States was building the Convair B-36, a bomber designed to fly from America to targets on the other side of the world and back without landing. Its wingspan was about 70 metres, 230 feet. That's wider than a Boeing 747's.

The B-36 could fly for well over a day. Fighters couldn't. The early jets drank fuel so fast that most could stay up for only an hour or two. No escort could follow the bomber more than a fraction of the way.

Every bomber crew of the last war knew what that meant. In World War Two, bombers flying beyond fighter cover had taken terrible losses.

So the Air Force asked a strange question. If a fighter can't follow the bomber… why not let the bomber carry the fighter?

It sounds absurd, but it had been done before. In the 1930s the US Navy flew giant airships that launched and caught small biplane fighters on a trapeze, in mid-air.

The Soviet Union went further. Its "Zveno" experiments carried fighters under a large bomber, and in 1941 the combination flew real combat missions.

So the obvious answer is: it had no landing gear because it didn't need a runway. But that's only half the story. The real reason the Goblin has no wheels comes down to one number. The Air Force gave McDonnell a box to fit: no more than about 15 feet long.

The fighter had to fit inside the B-36, in a space designed for bombs. Everything that couldn't fit had to go.

The result was 14 feet 10 inches, just over 4.5 metres, about the length of a family car.

The wings folded upward so it could slide into the bay, then swung down once it was clear. Spread out, they were about 6.4 metres across, 21 feet.

The tail was so cramped that the designers split it into multiple small fins, sticking out in every direction, to get enough control from almost no length.

Inside that egg was a single Westinghouse J34 jet engine, the same family used in larger fighters, with about 3,000 pounds of thrust pushing a jet that weighed around 2 tonnes, 4,500 pounds, fully loaded. On paper it could pass 1,000 kilometres an hour, about 650 miles an hour.

It was designed to carry four .50-calibre machine guns. It was small, but it was a real fighter.

And the pilot sat in a seat with an ejection system, looking up through his canopy at the steel hook that was his only way home.

Landing gear would have meant wheels, struts, brakes and hydraulics, adding weight and length to an aircraft that was already squeezed into a box. So the engineers took it out. In its place: one retractable hook, and a steel skid underneath for emergencies.

The B-36 wasn't ready to be converted, so the tests used a modified B-29 with a trapeze hanging from its bomb bay. It was nicknamed "Monstro," after the whale that swallowed Pinocchio.

The pilot was McDonnell test pilot Edwin Schoch. His job sounded simple: drop away, fly for a while, then come back up and put the hook on a bar swinging under a bomber in flight.

On the 23rd of August 1948, Schoch dropped free.

But the air under a four-engine bomber isn't still. It churns and surges, and it pushes anything small away from the aircraft above.

Schoch closed in. The Goblin bounced. The trapeze swung…

…and the bar hit his canopy. The glass shattered, and the blast of air tore his helmet and oxygen mask off.

With no wheels and no way back aboard, Schoch did the only thing left. He flew down to the dry lakebed and landed the fighter on its belly skid. He survived.

Weeks later, in October 1948, a Goblin finally hooked back onto its mothership in flight. It could be done. The trouble was doing it reliably.

Over the whole programme there were seven free flights. Only three ended with a successful hook-up. The other flights ended the same way the first had, with an emergency landing in the desert. These were test pilots in clear California weather, in daylight, with nobody shooting at them.

Now imagine that at night, in bad weather, over enemy territory, with fuel running out and nowhere to land if you miss.

By 1949 the Air Force had a better idea. Instead of carrying the fighter, bring fuel to the fighter. Mid-air refuelling meant ordinary fighters, with ordinary landing gear, could go much further.

The Goblin programme was cancelled. Only two were ever built.

Both survive today, one at the National Museum of the US Air Force in Ohio, the other in Nebraska.

The idea didn't quite die. In the 1950s the Air Force tried it again, with a full-size reconnaissance jet hanging under a modified B-36. And today, the idea of a big aircraft carrying and catching small ones is back again. This time the small ones are drones, with no pilot hanging under a hook.

Which brings us back to the question we started with. Why did this fighter have no landing gear?

Because its landing gear wasn't meant to be on the fighter at all. The Goblin's runway, hangar, fuel stop and way home were all meant to be the bomber above it. Every wheel, strut and brake it didn't carry was space and weight it could give to the only mission that mattered: fitting inside the B-36 and flying with it to the other side of the world.

And that's also why it failed. By giving up its own way home, the Goblin bet everything on one moment: catching a bar swinging under a bomber in rough air. In the desert, in daylight, that bet failed more often than it worked.

Landing gear would have made the Goblin heavier. Having none made it helpless.

Today, the Goblin in Ohio sits on a stand, its hook still raised, reaching for a bomber that will never come back for it.

That's what this channel is about. One aircraft. One strange design choice. And the physics that explains it.

And the Goblin isn't the strangest one we'll rebuild. There's a fighter that took off standing on its tail, and its pilot had to land it looking over his shoulder.
