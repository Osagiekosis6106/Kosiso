### Part 1 — The problem (≈3:20)

Edwards Air Force Base. 1955.

A ground crew starts the engine of a brand-new fighter. And within minutes the control tower is flashing a red light at them.

Not because something's wrong with the plane. Because the tower can't hear its own radios.

After one engine run, the crew chief is found on the floor of a nearby transport plane, arms and legs flailing, knocked down by the noise.

This is the Republic XF-84H. The Thunderscreech. Guinness still lists it as the loudest aircraft ever built.

But here's the strange part. The noise didn't come from the engine. It came from the propeller. A propeller that, once it started, never slowed down.

Not on the ground. Not at idle. Not in flight. It turned at the same speed the entire time the engine ran.

So why would anyone build a propeller like that? And why did that one decision make it the loudest machine in the sky — and doom it?

And the number the internet loves to quote is almost nine times too high. We're going to do the maths.

It starts with a problem every jet pilot in the early fifties knew.

Jet engines were slow to respond. Push the throttle forward, and the turbine had to spin up before the thrust arrived. Seconds. On a long runway, fine. On the short deck of an aircraft carrier, seconds were the difference between flying and going into the sea.

The U.S. Navy wanted a carrier fighter that could get off the deck without a catapult. The Air Force's Wright Air Development Center picked up the idea and became its main sponsor.

And the answer they reached for was the propeller.

A propeller bites the air instantly. Change the angle of the blades, and the thrust changes the same moment. No waiting for a turbine.

So Republic took its F-84F Thunderstreak, a swept-wing jet fighter, and rebuilt it around a turboprop.

The engine was an Allison XT40: five thousand eight hundred and fifty horsepower, that's about four thousand three hundred and sixty kilowatts, driving one huge propeller in the nose. Twelve feet across — three point seven metres — with three broad, square-tipped steel blades.

Two were built. And the way that engine was meant to be flown is the whole story.

### Part 2 — The physics (≈3:10)

Here's the trick.

In a car, you press the pedal and the engine speeds up. The XF-84H didn't work like that.

The turbine ran at one fixed speed: about fourteen thousand three hundred r-p-m. A gearbox stepped that down by six point eight to one. So the propeller turned at about two thousand one hundred r-p-m, all the time.

To get more thrust, the pilot didn't spin the propeller faster. He twisted the blades. Flat blades slice the air and push almost nothing. Twist them, and each blade grabs a bigger bite. Instant thrust, no spool-up. That was the point.

But a fixed speed has a price. And it shows up at the tips.

A propeller tip travels much farther than the hub on every turn. This one is twelve feet across, so the tip covers almost thirty-eight feet — about eleven and a half metres — every revolution.

At two thousand one hundred r-p-m, that's thirty-five turns every second.

Thirty-eight feet, thirty-five times a second. About thirteen hundred feet per second. Roughly four hundred metres a second.

The speed of sound at sea level is about eleven hundred and sixteen feet per second.

Which means the tips were moving at about Mach one point one eight — while the plane was standing still.

Every blade tip was supersonic on the ramp, at idle, with the brakes on.

And anything moving faster than sound drags a shock wave behind it. Each of the three tips was dragging its own shock wave around and around the nose.

Now the maths.

You'll see it everywhere: "nine hundred sonic booms per second." Let's check.

Three blades. Thirty-five turns a second. Three times thirty-five is one hundred and five.

So if you stood beside it, a shock went past you about a hundred and five times a second. That matches what Guinness says: "more than a hundred a second."

Not nine hundred. A hundred and five.

But here's why that's still terrible. A hundred and five pulses a second isn't a series of bangs. Your ear hears it as one continuous, brutal tone. A screech. Which is exactly what the crews called it.

And that same torque the propeller was putting into the air was trying to roll the whole aircraft the other way.

So Republic built the XF-84H crooked on purpose. The left air intake sat about a foot ahead of the right. The flaps on each wing were set differently. And the tail was moved up into a T, so the tailplane sat above the spiralling wash from the propeller, in clean air. An extra fin was even added behind the cockpit.

All of that, just to make it fly straight.

### Part 3 — The verdict (≈3:30)

On the twenty-second of July, 1955, Republic test pilot Henry "Hank" Beaird took it up for the first time.

It flew. Fast, for a propeller plane — about five hundred and twenty miles an hour, unofficially, around eight hundred and forty kilometres an hour. But it never came close to supersonic.

And it rarely came home normally.

Between July 1955 and October 1956, the two XF-84Hs made twelve test flights. According to the National Museum of the U.S. Air Force, eleven of them ended in emergency landings.

Twelve flights. Six hours and forty minutes in the air, in total.

Republic's other test pilot, Lin Hendrix, flew it once. Above about four hundred and fifty knots, he reported, it began to snake and lost its stability. He refused to fly it again.

On the ground, the noise was its own problem. Beaird remembered the day his crew chief, Edward von Wolffersdorff, was found on the floor of a C-47 after an engine run, flailing, knocked down by the noise. He recovered.

The tower kept flashing its red light because it couldn't hear its own radios. So the engine runs were moved to the far side of the base.

In the end, the Thunderscreech wasn't cancelled for being loud. It was cancelled because it didn't work well enough. Aerodynamic problems. An unreliable engine. Constant maintenance. And jet engines were getting better every year.

The problem it was built to solve was solving itself.

So why did the Thunderscreech's propeller never slow down?

Because that was the only way to get instant thrust. Hold the engine at one speed, twist the blades, and the power arrives the moment you ask for it.

But hold a twelve-foot propeller at two thousand one hundred r-p-m, and the tips break the sound barrier before the wheels have even turned. The same idea that gave it instant power made it the loudest aircraft ever built.

The first prototype survived. In 1999, the National Museum of the U.S. Air Force collected it from Kern County, California, and today it stands in Dayton, Ohio. Silent.

But the XF-84H wasn't the only aircraft built around this engine.

The same Allison T40 family powered another fighter that tried something even stranger: it took off standing on its tail — and its pilot had to land it looking backwards.
